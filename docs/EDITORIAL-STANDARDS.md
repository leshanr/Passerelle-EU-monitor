# Editorial standards

The rules that make this a publication rather than an aggregator.

---

## The test every item has to pass

> **Why should a young person care?**

Not "why is this important" — importance is cheap and the Commission publishes
forty important things a fortnight. The question is whether a 22-year-old in
London, Lisbon or Ljubljana is better off for having read it.

If the honest answer is *they shouldn't*, cut it. Cutting is the job. Twenty
items flagged, five published.

---

## Voice

Intelligent and politically literate, without sounding academic, bureaucratic or
like an EU press release.

**Write like this**
- "From 2027 you will get compensated for a three-hour delay instead of a
  five-hour one. Airlines fought this for eleven years."
- "The Court has just told every landlord association in Europe that rent caps
  are legal. That fight is over."
- "This looks like it points toward X, though the text is ambiguous on Y."

**Not like this**
- "Stakeholders welcomed the adoption of the framework."
- "This represents a significant milestone in the Union's ongoing efforts to..."
- "It remains to be seen what the implications will be."

Rules of thumb:

1. **No institutional nouns as subjects.** Not "the framework provides" but
   "from March, platforms have to".
2. **Numbers and dates beat adjectives.** "12% cut" beats "significant cut".
3. **Name the people.** "Stakeholders" is not a person. "German carmakers" is.
4. **Explain the acronym once, then use it.** Never assume, never patronise.
5. **Be honest about uncertainty.** Hedging accurately reads as analysis.
   Overclaiming reads as a student blog.
6. **Short sentences carry hard ideas.** If a sentence has three commas, it is
   two sentences.

## Structure of an edition

| Element | Budget | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Opener | 60 words | What the fortnight was *about*. Written last. |
| The big one | 400–500 words | The lead development, with real analysis. |
| 4–7 shorter items | 120–200 words each | Explainer, not summary. Every one answers "why care". |
| One number | 1 chart / map / stat | The most-shared thing you will publish. Twenty minutes' work. |
| And finally | 80 words | One lighter or genuinely odd European development. |

Total 1,200–1,800 words. About a ten-minute read. A reader should finish it
thinking *I actually understand what has been happening in Europe.*

---

## Sourcing

1. **Every claim links to a primary source.** The digest already carries the
   links. This is what separates this from an aggregator.
2. **Read the primary source in full** before writing about it. The press
   release is not the regulation.
3. **Find one independent account** of anything you lead with. If nobody else
   has reported it, either you have a scoop or you have misread it, and the
   second is more likely.
4. **Distinguish proposal from law.** "The Commission has proposed" and "this is
   now binding" are different stories and readers cannot tell them apart unless
   you do it for them.
5. **Never write from the feed summary alone.** The summary exists to help you
   decide whether to open the link.

---

## Corrections

Wrong is fine. Quietly wrong is not.

A correction goes at the top of the next edition, named as a correction, saying
what was wrong and what is right. No edits to a published piece without a note
saying it was edited. This costs nothing and is the whole basis on which anyone
trusts a one-person publication.

## What the automation may and may not do

| Machine | Human |
|---|---|
| Fetch and parse feeds | Decide what is worth publishing |
| Discard what has been seen | Research the primary source |
| Score against beats and signals | Judge significance |
| Rank and flag | Write every word |
| Report source health | Decide what to cut |

The system never writes copy. It says *this looks potentially significant*, and
that is a different sentence from *this is worth your attention*. Every published
word is written by a person who has read the source.

This is stated on the record, in the repo and at the foot of every edition,
because the distinction is the credibility of the whole project. A publication
that quietly generates its copy has nothing to offer that a feed reader does not.

---

## Independence

- No sponsored items, ever, without a label at the top.
- Collaborations with university societies are labelled as collaborations.
- The repo is public. Anyone can check what the system flagged and see what was
  cut. That is a feature.
